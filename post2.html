In March 2026, Pew Research asked working parents a simple question: do you spend enough time with your children?

56 out of 100 said no. Too little time. And almost two out of three said work is the reason.

65% say work is the reason

Read that again. More than half of us feel we are missing it.

These are not lazy parents. These are parents who leave home before the kids wake up and walk back in after dark. Parents who miss dinners, games, and bedtime stories because the job does not wait.

And here is the part that hurts more.

A 2026 survey found that parents spend 38 minutes every single day fighting with their kids — about cleaning rooms, getting ready, homework.

That adds up to 10 full days a year. Gone to arguing.

THE DAILY FIGHT · 2026
38 minutes a day spent fighting with the kids

Another survey found that 9 out of 10 parents argue about screen time. Half of them fight about it every week.

SCREEN TIME · 2026
9 in 10 parents argue with their kids over screen time

So the problem is not only that we have too few hours. Too many of the hours we do have go to fighting.

I know this one in my bones.

I am a father of six. For years I drove Uber and worked around 100 hours a week. I came home tired, and tired made me short. During COVID, I was not present enough for my older kids. That is a regret I carry.

My parents were fighting to keep us alive. I am fighting to keep my children connected.

I cannot get more hours in my day. But I can stop wasting the ones I have.

I stopped trying to find more time. Here is what I am doing with the time I have instead.

Here is what I am trying. Not what worked — I am still in the middle of it.

First, we are building a family agreement. My son and I sit down, he suggests rules, I suggest mine, and we review it together. It is an experiment. He has not even given me his full feedback yet. But the sitting down together — that part is already worth it.

Second, when I am with my kids, I am trying to leave my phone in another room. Not always. I fail at this. But I am trying, because every time I check it, I am telling them the phone matters more than they do.

Third, when I snap after a long day — and I do — I apologize. Saying sorry did not make my authority disappear. It made me honest.

The surveys will keep coming, and the numbers will keep saying the same thing: parents are running out of time. I cannot fix the job, and I cannot fix the clock.

But ten minutes of real attention is worth more than a whole evening in the same room with my face in a screen. That is what I am fighting for now — not more hours, but better ones.
